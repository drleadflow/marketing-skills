# The AI-tell checklist

Run this before calling a draft finished. The goal isn't to hunt down every item below on sight, it's to notice when one of them turns into a habit. A single instance rarely bothers a reader; the same move repeated three times in one piece is what makes a draft feel machine-written. When a business has a documented voice that genuinely uses one of these patterns on purpose, keep it. This list targets the defaults a model reaches for without being asked, not a deliberate stylistic choice.

## Vocabulary that gives it away

Certain words show up in generated text far more often than in how people actually talk: delve, leverage used as a verb, utilize, robust, seamless, streamline, unlock, elevate, navigate when it means "deal with" rather than literal navigation, landscape when it means "the general situation," ecosystem, synergy, holistic, game-changer, pivotal, cutting-edge, and the phrase "in today's world." None of these are banned outright in every context, but if the plain, spoken-aloud version of the word exists, use that one instead. "We fixed the signup flow" beats "we streamlined the onboarding experience."

Watch for verbs that add weight without adding meaning: "serves as," "functions as," "stands as a testament to," where a plain "is" already does the job. "The dashboard is where the team checks numbers" says more than "the dashboard serves as the team's central hub for data."

Adverbs that inflate a plain sentence into something that sounds profound: quietly, deeply, fundamentally, remarkably. If removing the adverb doesn't change the truth of the sentence, it wasn't doing any work.

Claims with no name attached: "studies show," "experts agree," "research suggests." A sentence that can't name its source isn't citing anything, it's borrowing the sound of authority. Either name the source or drop the claim.

## Sentence shapes that repeat too often

**The flip.** "It's not about speed, it's about trust." One of these per article is fine, maybe even good. A paragraph that leans on this shape three or four times starts to feel like a pattern the writer fell into rather than a point being made.

**Rhetorical question into a clipped answer.** "So what actually matters here? Consistency." This works once. A page that opens three different sections with a question and a one-word answer reads like a template, not like someone thinking out loud.

**Matching triplets.** "Faster. Cheaper. Easier." A triplet lands when it's earned; stacking several triplets back to back across a page starts to sound like a pitch deck rather than an explanation.

**The "-ing" tail.** Adding a dangling clause to manufacture significance: "...cementing its place as the industry standard," "...paving the way for what comes next." Often the sentence is stronger if it just stops at the actual fact.

**Announcing before saying.** "It's worth noting that response times improved." Cut "it's worth noting that" and the sentence is identical, just shorter and more direct.

## Paragraph-level habits

Vary sentence length on purpose. A paragraph where every sentence runs 18 to 22 words reads evenly, which sounds fine in theory and flat in practice; real writing has a short sentence next to a longer one. Also avoid starting three sentences in a row with the same word: "The tool logs errors. The tool flags spikes. The tool emails the team." Break the pattern: "The tool logs errors and flags spikes. The team gets an email the moment one crosses the threshold."

Watch for a list that's secretly hiding inside a paragraph: "First, there's the cost. Then there's the setup time. Finally, there's the learning curve." If it's a list, format it as one. If it's not, write it as connected prose with real transitions instead of numbered connective words.

## Tone habits that read as performance rather than voice

Manufactured suspense: "Here's the part nobody talks about." "But here's the twist." Say the thing. The drumroll isn't adding tension, it's delaying the point.

Performed candor: "I'll be honest, this surprised me." "Not going to sugarcoat it." Real honesty shows up in specifics, not in announcing that honesty is coming.

Oversized stakes for an ordinary claim: "this changes everything about how teams collaborate." Match the size of the claim to the size of the evidence behind it.

Raising an objection just to wave it away: "Sure, there's a learning curve, but the payoff is worth it." Either actually address the objection with specifics, or don't raise it at all.

## Formatting defaults to avoid

Em dashes and en dashes used as connective tissue in place of a comma, a period, or a colon. One here and there is invisible. A paragraph with four or five of them reads as a tic.

Every bullet point opening with a bolded label followed by a colon. It's a fast way to format a list, and it's also the first thing a reader notices as a template. Mix in plain-sentence bullets, or write the list as prose.

Decorative arrows and curly-quote characters standing in for words ("→" instead of "leads to"). Spell it out.

Headings that say nothing about what's actually in the section: "Overview," "Conclusion," "Key Takeaways." A heading should tell the reader what they'll learn there, specifically.

Leaning on tables to dress up a point that's really just one sentence of prose. Save a table for information that's genuinely comparative or tabular.

## Whole-piece patterns

Telling the reader what's coming, delivering it, then summarizing what was just delivered, at every level of the piece from the intro down to each section. Say the thing once, not three times in three different wrappers.

One image or metaphor introduced early and then dragged through the entire piece, showing up in every section whether it still fits or not. Use it once where it helps, then let it go.

Citing the same type of example repeated for effect: "Company A did this. Company B did this. Company C did this too." A single, well-chosen example usually does more work than a parade of shallow ones.

Making the same point twice in different words later in the piece, which is a common sign of a draft that was generated in one long pass and never trimmed. Read the piece end to end looking specifically for this.

## Titles

Lead with what the reader actually gets, keep it short enough to show in full on a search result, and promise one real, specific outcome rather than a vague improvement. Skip the title shapes that signal a template on sight: "The Ultimate Guide to X," "Everything You Need to Know About X," "X: A Complete Guide." If the title promises a number ("7 Ways to..."), the body needs to deliver exactly that number, not six or eight.

## Openings

Three to five sentences is usually enough. Skip the stock openers that every reader has seen a thousand times: "In today's fast-paced world," "Whether you're a beginner or an expert," "Have you ever wondered." Start with the actual point or the actual problem instead of a runway leading up to it.

## Closings

Land the final point without flagging that it's the final point. Skip "in conclusion," "to sum up," "at the end of the day." If the piece needs a closing section, give it one real idea to land on rather than a recap of every section that came before.

## A fast self-check before shipping

Search the draft for: em dashes, "it's not," "here's the thing," "in conclusion," "delve," and any heading that could apply to literally any article on the internet. Read three consecutive sentences out loud, start to finish, and listen for whether they all start the same way or run the same length. Ask whether any single sentence could be lifted into a competitor's article on the same topic without anyone noticing; if so, it's generic enough to cut or sharpen. And check whether any point gets made twice in different clothes.
