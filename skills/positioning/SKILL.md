# Positioning

You'll finish with a one-page **Positioning Sheet**: the alternatives your customer is really choosing between, the one difference you'll own, your method named and drawn as three pillars, the six beliefs a prospect must hold before they buy, a positioning statement, a one-liner and a ten-second spoken version. Every ad, page and script repeats it, so the market hears one thing.

Positioning isn't a slogan. It's the answer to "why you, why not the alternative, why now" that a prospect can repeat to their spouse. Most businesses lose not to better providers but to better-explained ones.

## Before you start

- Read `.agents/business-context.md` and `.agents/proof-ledger.md` if they exist. Section 3 (best client) is required. If it's thin, run **best-client-profile** first.
- If business-context is missing, offer to run **business-context** first, or ask:
  1. What do you sell, and to whom?
  2. Who else does your customer consider, including doing it themselves or doing nothing?
  3. What do your best customers say made them choose you?
- Ask for 3 to 10 competitor websites or profiles, and anything customers have said comparing you to them.

## How to do it

### 1. Map the real alternatives

Your customer isn't only comparing you with similar businesses. List all three kinds:

| Alternative | Example | What it gets right, in customer words | Where it fails, in customer words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct competitors | Other firms that do what you do | | |
| Do it themselves | DIY, software, a cheaper helper, a family member | | |
| Do nothing | Wait, live with it, "next quarter" | | |

"Do nothing" is usually the biggest competitor. If you can't say why waiting costs them, you can't beat it.

### 2. Scan the competitors

For each direct competitor, record from their public material (never invent it):

- Who they say they're for.
- Their main promise.
- Their method, if named.
- Their proof.
- Their entry offer and price, if public.
- How they sound.

Then build a whitespace table. Rows are attributes; count how many competitors claim each.

| Attribute | What's claimed | How many claim it | Crowded or open |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who it's for | | | |
| Main promise | | | |
| Method | | | |
| Proof type | | | |
| Speed | | | |
| Delivery (done for you, with you, by you) | | | |
| Price position | | | |
| Voice | | | |

"Crowded" is 3 or more claiming it. Open rows are where a difference can live. Copy a competitor's structure if you like, never their claims or words.

### 3. Choose one difference

Score candidate differences 1 to 3 on each test. Keep the one with the highest total, and nothing under 2 on any test.

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| True | Is it true today, not after the rebrand? |
| Matters | Does the best client care, in their words? |
| Provable | Is there proof in the ledger, or can there be within 90 days? |
| Open | Is the row open or weak in the whitespace table? |
| Hard to copy | Would a competitor need more than a new headline to claim it? |

Strong differences are usually about **how** you do it (a method, a sequence, a standard you hold), **who** it's for (a narrow group you know deeply) or **what you refuse to do**. Weak ones are adjectives: quality, care, experience, passion.

### 4. Write the wedge

One or two lines that reframe the problem so your difference becomes the obvious answer. Shape:

> You're not losing to [what they blame]. You're losing to [the real cause you fix].

Fictional examples:
- Home services: "You're not losing jobs to cheaper plumbers. You're losing them to plumbers who call back first."
- Coach: "Your business isn't stuck because you need more ideas. It's stuck because nobody looks at the same three numbers every Monday."

The wedge names an enemy that is a belief, habit or method, never a named competitor or person, and never the customer.

### 5. Name the method and draw it

Give your way of working a plain, ownable name and three pillars. Each pillar fixes one problem the customer names and has three parts.

```
<METHOD NAME>
Pillar 1: <name>       solves "<problem in their words>"
  - part  - part  - part
Pillar 2: <name>       solves "<problem in their words>"
  - part  - part  - part
Pillar 3: <name>       solves "<problem in their words>"
  - part  - part  - part
```

Rules for the name: describes what it does, not a promise of a result; no guarantee words, no trademarks you don't own, no borrowed framework names. "The Callback-First System" works. "The Guaranteed Growth Formula" doesn't.

This triangle becomes a graphic, a carousel, a sales-call whiteboard and the outline of the offer.

### 6. Map the six beliefs

Before anyone buys, they must believe six things. One unresolved belief is enough for them to go quiet. For each, write their current belief in their words, the new belief, and how you'll shift it (an argument, a demonstration, a proof item from the ledger).

| # | Belief to install | Their current belief (their words) | How we shift it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The problem is real, and deeper than the symptom I see | | |
| 2 | This method is the fastest path to what I want | | |
| 3 | Now is the time: waiting costs more than acting | | |
| 4 | It's worth the money: a return, not a cost | | |
| 5 | It beats the other options I'm weighing (competitors, doing it myself, doing nothing) | | |
| 6 | I don't have to do it alone: a guide gets me there faster | | |

This is the one six-beliefs list for the whole repo. Skills that write belief-shift content (ad-angles-and-copy, content-pillars) use the same six, in this order.

Shift beliefs by giving new information and validating why the old belief made sense, never by calling the old belief stupid. Price the cost of waiting honestly; never invent urgency.

Set the order by how many similar offers the market has already seen:

- **Crowded market** (they've seen dozens of these offers): skip explaining the category; lead with your difference and trust in you.
- **Middle**: lead with what's different about your method, then build trust.
- **New category** (they barely know it exists): first prove the method works at all, then the rest.

### 7. Write the statements

```
Positioning statement (internal):
For <best client> who <trigger moment>, <business> is the <category>
that <difference>, so they <result in their words>.
Unlike <main alternative>, which <where it fails>.

One-liner (site header, bio):
<who> + <result> + <how, briefly>

Ten-second spoken version (how the owner answers "what do you do?"):
<one or two sentences, the way they'd say it at a barbecue>
```

Also write **What we're not**: 3 to 5 things you deliberately don't do. This makes the difference believable.

### 8. Test it

- **Five-second test:** show the one-liner to someone who matches the best client. Can they say who it's for and what they'd get? If not, rewrite.
- **Competitor swap:** put a competitor's name in your statement. If it still reads true, it's not a difference.
- **Proof check:** every claim in the sheet is in the proof ledger or marked `[NEEDS PROOF: ...]`.

### 9. Write it down

Deliver the Positioning Sheet (all of the above on one page). Then write `### Positioning` in section 4 of `.agents/business-context.md`: the one-liner, the difference, who you're the obvious choice for, the method name and pillars. Append a changelog line.

## Check it

| # | Check | Pass when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three alternatives | Direct, DIY and do-nothing all mapped, in customer words |
| 2 | Whitespace | Competitor table built from their real public material |
| 3 | One difference | Scored on all five tests, none under 2 |
| 4 | Wedge | Names a belief or method as the enemy, not a person or the customer |
| 5 | Method | Named, three pillars, each pillar solves a problem in customer words |
| 6 | Six beliefs | All six rows filled, with a way to shift each |
| 7 | Statements | Statement, one-liner, spoken version and "what we're not" all written |
| 8 | Swap test | The statement fails when a competitor's name is swapped in |
| 9 | Proof | Every claim maps to the ledger or a visible [NEEDS PROOF] |
| 10 | Clean | No guarantee or hype words; no em or en dashes |

## Rules

- Never claim a difference that isn't true today.
- Never attack or name a competitor in public copy. Contrast with a method or a habit.
- Never copy a competitor's claims, slogans or method names. Learn from the structure, write your own.
- Never use someone else's trademarked or branded framework name for your method.
- Never position on "cheapest". Someone will always be cheaper.
- No shame, blame or "you should have known" framing toward the customer.
- In regulated industries, the difference describes what you do, not a health, money or legal outcome. Check section 10 of business-context and **health-claims-check** where it applies.

## Ask your audience

1. Who else did you look at before choosing us?
2. Why didn't you go with them?
3. What did we say or do that made you pick us?
4. What would you have done if we didn't exist?
5. How would you describe what we do to a friend?

## Examples

A fictional home-services company, condensed:

```markdown
**Wedge:** You're not losing jobs to cheaper plumbers. You're losing them to plumbers who call back first.
**Difference:** We call back within 10 minutes during business hours and give a written price before any work starts.
**Method:** The Straight Price Visit
  - Fast: callback in minutes, 2-hour arrival window, a text when the tech is 20 minutes out
  - Seen: camera inspection, footage shown on the spot
  - Fixed: written price before work, no add-ons without a new signature
**One-liner:** Plumbing repairs with the price in writing before we start.
**Spoken:** "We're the plumbers who call you back fast and tell you the price before we touch anything."
**What we're not:** not the cheapest; no "starting at" prices; no work without a written price; no upsell inspections.
```
