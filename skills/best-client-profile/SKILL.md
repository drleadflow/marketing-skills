# Best client profile

You'll finish with a **Best Client Profile**: one real type of person described so clearly that a new team member could pick them out of a list; the moment that makes them look for help; what they've already tried; what they believe; who they trust; the before and after in their exact words; the signs of a client to turn away; and the three things your best clients do that make them succeed. It goes into section 3 of `.agents/business-context.md`, and every skill that writes copy aims at it.

Write to one person, not a demographic. "Women 35 to 55 interested in wellness" is a targeting setting. "A dental office manager who's been told to 'fix the schedule' and has no budget to do it" is a person you can write to.

## Before you start

- Read `.agents/business-context.md` and `.agents/proof-ledger.md` if they exist. If business-context is missing, offer to run **business-context** first, or ask:
  1. What do you sell, and what does it cost?
  2. Who's the favorite customer you've ever had, and why?
  3. Who's a customer you'd never take again, and why?
- Ask for raw customer language: sales call recordings or notes, intake forms, emails and DMs from prospects, reviews, the questions people ask before they buy. Ten real conversations beat any amount of guessing.

## How to do it

### 1. Start from a real person

Ask the owner to picture their best client ever: someone who paid well, got a great result, was easy to work with and sent referrals. Then ask:

> Imagine they're sitting across from you. What do they do all day? What was going on the week they first contacted you? What had they already tried? What did they say on the first call?

Push past categories once, warmly: "That describes a lot of people. Which one person were you thinking of?" Build from that person, then check the profile still fits 2 or 3 other good clients. If it only fits one, it's an anecdote; keep looking.

Never write down the real person's name or identifying details. Describe the pattern.

### 2. Fill the profile

Use this structure. Every line in the "their words" fields is a quote or close paraphrase from real conversations, marked `(quote)`, `(paraphrase)` or `(owner's guess)`.

**What they type or ask:** <the exact searches or questions, e.g. "why do my leads not answer the phone">
**Already tried (at least 3):**

### 3. Find the trigger moment

The trigger is where marketing meets the client. People don't buy when they have a problem; they buy when the problem shows up in a way they can't ignore. Ask: "What happened the week before they called?" Good triggers are concrete events: a bad month, a staff member quitting, a wedding date set, a failed inspection, a photo they didn't like, a competitor opening nearby. Aim content and ads at that moment.

### 4. Name the before and after identities

People buy a change in who they are, not a service. Name both sides:

- **Before**: something they'd be embarrassed to say out loud. ("The owner who's also the receptionist.")
- **After**: something they'd put in their bio. ("The owner who runs it from the numbers.")

Test: would a real client wince at the first and want the second? If neither lands, try again with their words.

### 5. Write the screen-out list

The best-client profile is half about who you turn away. From the owner's worst clients, list 5 to 8 concrete signs, for example:

- Wants it done for them and never looks at the numbers.
- Won't give access to what you need on day one.
- Isn't the decision maker, or the decision maker skips the calls.
- Has paid for nothing to fix this in the last year.
- Is waiting for a better time (the renovation, the slow season, the new hire).
- Blames every previous provider.
- Asks for a discount before asking what's included.

Every sign must be something a setter or a form question could detect.

### 6. Find the three success behaviors

Ask: "What did your best clients do that your average ones didn't?" Usually it's behavior, not demographics: gave access fast, had one person answering the phone, showed up to every session, did the homework. These become qualifying questions and onboarding requirements.

### 7. Check it with real people

Show the profile to one person on the team and take one round of edits. If the profile feels generic, stop writing and talk to three real clients using the questions under "Ask your audience". Then update.

### 8. Write it down

Write section 3 of `.agents/business-context.md` (keep the `### In their words` subsection with the before and after quotes), and append a changelog line. Redo the profile once a year, or when the offer changes.

## Check it

| # | Check | Pass when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One person | The portrait describes one recognizable person, not an age range and an interest |
| 2 | Fits several | It fits at least 2 or 3 real past clients |
| 3 | Real words | At least 5 quotes or paraphrases, labeled, in the before and after fields |
| 4 | Trigger | The trigger is a specific event with a time, not a general pain |
| 5 | Tried | At least 3 things they already tried |
| 6 | Trust | Named sources of trust, not "industry experts" |
| 7 | Identities | Before passes the embarrassment test; after passes the bio test |
| 8 | Screen-out | 5 or more signs, each detectable by a question or behavior |
| 9 | Success behaviors | 3 behaviors that predict success |
| 10 | Private | No real names or identifying details; owner guesses labeled as guesses |

## Rules

- Never invent customer quotes. Label anything that isn't a real quote.
- Never include a real client's name, photo, health detail or anything that identifies them.
- Never describe the client in a way that shames them, in the profile or in copy built from it. The before identity is for internal clarity; copy shows the problem without telling the reader what's wrong with them.
- Don't widen the profile to include everyone who might buy. Narrow wins; the business can still serve others.
- A handful of conversations is a starting point, not proof of what the market thinks. Say how many conversations the profile is built on.

## Ask your audience

Ask 3 to 5 recent clients:

1. What was going on the week you first reached out?
2. What had you tried before us, and what was wrong with it?
3. What almost stopped you from buying?
4. What's different now, in your own words?
5. If you were telling a friend about us, what would you say?

## Examples

A short profile for a fictional med spa (invented for illustration):

```markdown
**Who they are:** A working parent in their early 40s who manages a team at work and runs the household calendar. They've started noticing they look tired in video calls, even when they aren't.
**Name we use internally:** "Busy Bree"
**Trigger moment:** A photo from a family event that they untagged themselves from.
**What they type or ask:** "natural looking results" "will people be able to tell"
**Already tried:** expensive eye creams; a discount place a friend used (felt rushed); "just sleeping more"
**The quiet belief:** "If I do anything, I'll look done."
**Who they trust:** two friends who've had treatments; one dermatologist podcast; reviews that mention "natural"
**Before, in their words:** "I look exhausted all the time." (quote) "I don't want to look like I had work done." (quote)
**After, in their words:** "People just say I look rested." (quote)
**Before identity:** the one hiding behind the camera
**After identity:** the one who's in the photos
**Screen-out signs:** price shopping by the unit on the first message; wants a specific result guaranteed; wants treatment on the same day without an assessment
```
